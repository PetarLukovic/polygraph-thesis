\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{Motivation}

Many robots, vehicles, and wearable devices do not see an environment once: they see it repeatedly. A delivery robot drives the same warehouse floor every day. A fleet of survey vehicles maps the same city over months. A person wearing an AR headset walks through the same building on many separate occasions. Each of these passes through the environment, which we call a \emph{session}, is tracked independently by the device's own onboard visual or visual-inertial odometry. Within a session, this odometry is locally accurate but accumulates drift over time, with drift growing worse as the area covered increases. In addition, every session's trajectory is expressed in its own local coordinate frame with no inherent relationship to any other session's frame. Two sessions that physically revisit the same corridor or intersection have no way, from odometry alone, of knowing that they did.

Recovering that relationship (detecting where sessions overlap, using those overlaps to correct each session's drift, and placing every session into one shared, globally consistent frame) is the multi-session trajectory optimization problem we want to tackle. Solving it well is what turns a pile of independent, drifting trajectory logs into a single coherent map of an environment, and it is a prerequisite for any system that wants to accumulate knowledge about a place across repeated visits rather than starting from zero every time. \myfigref{fig:intro-hydro-loop-closures} shows what that looks like once solved.

\section{Technical Gap}

The classical way to detect that two sessions overlap is to build and maintain a persistent 3D map (sparse or dense) and match new observations against it, as in multi-map SLAM systems such as ORB-SLAM3's Atlas \cite{elvira2019orbslamatlasrobustaccuratemultimap} or the collaborative back-end COVINS \cite{covins} and COVINS-G \cite{covins-g}. Maintaining such a map gives strong geometric evidence for a match, but it is computationally and storage heavy, especially when repeated optimization is necessary. Keeping and matching maps across many long sessions scales poorly, which limits deployments to a bounded number of sessions or a bounded environment size. Even where a map is built successfully, it is often not what downstream robotic modules actually need. Dense landmark clouds are rarely consumed directly by planning or navigation, which typically expect other representations entirely, so the cost of maintaining one is not always paid back by later use.

A recent line of work entirely avoids maintaining any such map. Lim et al.'s 2GO \cite{lim2025twogo} represents each session only as a sparse, keyframed pose graph where intra-session revisits are established with learned image retrieval and two-view geometry. Sparse feature matching is lifted to metric scale with a monocular depth prior, rather than any persistent map, and fused directly into the pose graph. This is substantially lighter than maintaining one and is the closest prior work to this thesis, as it is also intended as an online pipeline.

However, 2GO has only single-session capabilities and cannot exploit cross-session information. This information, as we show later, can be very useful, improving even strong onboard odometry from agents such as HoloLens or Spot.

\section{This Work}

This thesis presents \textbf{Polygraph}, a multi-session trajectory optimization pipeline that targets exactly this gap: extending 2GO's map-less, per-pair verification recipe from a single continuous capture to genuinely independent sessions, processed online. Polygraph processes each session's odometry keyframes incrementally, grouping them into local spatial clusters as they arrive. Whenever a cluster closes, it is matched against the keyframes of every other session through learned retrieval, and candidate cross-session matches are verified with the same sparse matching plus monocular depth approach as 2GO \cite{lim2025twogo}. Unlike 2GO, which reasons about one continuous capture, Polygraph makes no assumption that every session is known up front and designates no session a pre-fixed reference in advance: every session starts as its own independent graph component, and cross-session edges merge components together only once a two-tier consensus gate has been passed. The shared pose graph is kept current online through a cheap incremental solve after every closed cluster, and is periodically re-solved and pruned of outlier edges through a robust batch optimization pass. This lightweight optimization is possible due to the sparsity of our graph and the removal of any persistent map.

In short, this thesis contributes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An online formulation of sparse, map-less, retrieval-based pose-graph optimization, tested on a wide spectrum of agents, scenarios, and camera setups
  \item Multi-session map fusion using cross-session retrieval-based edges and cross-session information sharing for single-session accuracy improvement
  \item A robust cluster-matching and joint pose-estimation pipeline using monocular depth priors in combination with different learned image-matching methods
  \item A systems level characterization of the runtime, accuracy, and loop closure recall trade offs of online multi-session fusion, including a direct, hardware-matched comparison against 2GO \cite{lim2025twogo}
\end{itemize}
