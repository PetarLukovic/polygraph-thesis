This chapter describes Polygraph, the multi-session trajectory optimization pipeline this thesis
presents. \mysecref{sec:method-overview} states the problem formally and walks through, at a
high level, what happens to one keyframe and one cluster as they move through the pipeline.
\mysecref{sec:method-odometry}--\ref{sec:method-graph} then cover each pipeline stage in turn,
in the order data actually flows: keyframing, clustering, cluster matching, pose estimation,
the two-tier gate, and pose-graph optimization.

\section{Method Overview}
\label{sec:method-overview}
\subsection{Notation and Problem Setup}

Let $S = \{1, \dots, N\}$ index the $N$ sessions to be fused. Each session $s \in S$ is
tracked independently by a single-session visual(inertial) odometry backbone
(\mychapterref{chap:relatedwork} discusses several), producing a stream of raw per-frame poses
$T^{\mathrm{raw}}_{s,i} \in \SE{3}$, $i = 1, \dots, F_s$, expressed purely in session $s$'s own
local frame $\mathcal{F}_s$, drifting over time, and with no inherent relationship to any
other session's frame. \mysecref{sec:method-odometry} selects a keyframe subsequence
$K_s = (k_1 < k_2 < \dots < k_{|K_s|}) \subseteq \{1,\dots,F_s\}$. Write $T_{s,j} :=
T^{\mathrm{raw}}_{s,k_j}$ for the pose of the $j$-th keyframe of session $s$. Everything downstream of
\mysecref{sec:method-odometry} operates purely at the granularity of the keyframe.

Define a graph $G = (V, E)$ with one vertex per keyframe in every session,
$V = \{ (s,j) : s \in S,\ j = 1,\dots,|K_s| \}$, and edges $E = E_{\mathrm{odom}} \cup
E_{\mathrm{intra}} \cup E_{\mathrm{cross}}$ of three kinds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Odometry edges} $E_{\mathrm{odom}}$: one per consecutive keyframe pair
    $(s,j),(s,j{+}1)$ within a session, with measurement $z_{(s,j),(s,j+1)} = T_{s,j}^{-1}
    T_{s,j+1}$ taken directly from the raw odometry (trusted, affected by drift). Never removed
    by outlier rejection.
  \item \textbf{Intra-session edges} $E_{\mathrm{intra}}$: loop-closure edges between two
    \emph{non-consecutive} keyframes of the \emph{same} session, confirmed by the same
    retrieval-and-verification pipeline as cross-session edges
    (\mysecref{sec:method-cluster-matching}, \mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation}) but
    admitted directly, without the gate, since that session's position is already anchored
    by $E_{\mathrm{odom}}$ (\mysecref{sec:method-gate}).
  \item \textbf{Cross-session edges} $E_{\mathrm{cross}}$: between keyframes of two
    \emph{different} sessions, added only once a candidate match survives verification
    (\mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation}) and is admitted by the gate
    (\mysecref{sec:method-gate}). Measurement $\hat{z}_{(s,j),(s',j')} \in \SE{3}$ is the
    estimated relative pose between the two keyframes.
\end{itemize}
The connected components of $G$ partition $V$ into groups of sessions confirmed, with
corroborated evidence, that they would revisit each other. Sessions in different components do not have an
established relative pose. For every vertex $v = (s,j) \in V$, let $X_v \in \SE{3}$ be its
pose in the shared frame of its own component. The goal is to estimate $\{X_v\}_{v \in V}$, for
every component, by solving
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:method-pgo-objective}
  \min_{\{X_v\}_{v \in V}} \;
  \sum_{(u,v) \in E} \rho_{uv}\!\Big( r(X_u, X_v, z_{uv});\, \Sigma_{uv} \Big)
  \;+\; \sum_{v \in V_0} \big\| X_v \ominus p_v \big\|^2_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{prior}}},
\end{equation}
where $r(X_u,X_v,z) = \mathrm{Log}\!\big(z^{-1} X_u^{-1} X_v\big) \in \mathbb{R}^6$ is the
$\SE{3}$ residual under the standard Lie-algebra logarithm map, $\Sigma_{uv}$ is the edge's
noise covariance, and $\rho_{uv}$ a robust loss: identity for odometry edges (trusted and never
pruned), a Cauchy $M$-estimator for confirmed loop-closure edges (both intra- and
cross-session), or, in the final archival pass, a GNC-TLS weight (see
\mysecref{sec:method-graph}). $V_0 \subseteq V$ is the set of per-component gauge-fixing
anchor vertices, one per session, each with prior pose $p_v$.

Two properties of \eqref{eq:method-pgo-objective} are what distinguishes Polygraph from an
offline formulation of the same problem. First, $V$ and $E$ grow monotonically online, one keyframe and one confirmed edge at a time, without the assumption that $S$ or any session's full trajectory is known in advance. Secondly, $V_0$ is never fixed up front. Every session receives its own gauge-fixing prior the moment it starts (as its own, momentarily disconnected, single-vertex component), and components merge,
but never split, only when a cross-session edge survives verification and the gate.

Before the detailed, per-stage treatment in the rest of this chapter, it is worth tracing what
actually happens to one keyframe and one cluster as they move through Polygraph, end to end
(\myfigref{fig:method-overview}).

The raw odometry stream of a session is thinned to keyframes as it arrives
(\mysecref{sec:method-odometry}). Each new keyframe is appended to that session's own
currently-open \emph{cluster}, a short, spatially bounded run of keyframes
(\mysecref{sec:method-clustering}). Clusters, not individual keyframes, are the unit
everything downstream reasons about. A cluster only becomes visible to the rest of the
pipeline once it \emph{closes}, at which point every one of its member keyframes has already
been assigned a global image descriptor.

The moment a cluster closes, it is matched against the already closed clusters of every other session through image retrieval (\mysecref{sec:method-cluster-matching}). Its keyframes vote
for their nearest neighbors elsewhere, and a short list of candidate cluster pairs falls out. As unverified hypotheses, not yet trusted. Then each pair of candidates is geometrically checked (\mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation}). Only a handful of keyframe pairs from the two clusters are matched, triangulated, lifted to the metric scale with a monocular depth prior, and cross-checked against each other. A pair that survives this becomes one \emph{confirmed edge}, a single metric relative pose between two keyframes, one in each cluster.

A confirmed edge is not yet allowed into the shared pose graph unconditionally. If both
clusters belong to the same session, they commit immediately. That session's own position
is already fully known from its own sequential odometry, so this is just corroborating
evidence. If the two clusters belong to different sessions, the edge instead passes through
the two-tier consensus gate (\mysecref{sec:method-gate}), which decides, based on how much
prior cross-session evidence that session already has, whether this single piece of evidence
is enough to trust on its own or needs to be corroborated by another before it is allowed to
merge two session' components together.

Every edge that is ultimately admitted (intra or cross session) becomes a factor in the
pose graph of \eqref{eq:method-pgo-objective}, kept current by a cheap incremental solve after
every closed cluster and periodically refined by a more expensive batch solve that also prunes
edges that turn out to be outliers (\mysecref{sec:method-graph}). The result, once every
session has been processed, is one pose graph spanning every session shown, with corroborated
evidence, to revisit another, each session's own trajectory corrected wherever it overlaps
with the rest.


Unless stated otherwise, every numeric threshold quoted in this chapter is the production configuration used to generate this thesis's KITTI multi-session results, not necessarily the pipeline's own built-in code defaults, which are in several places more conservative. Every threshold remains freely configurable per dataset.

\section{Device Odometry and Keyframing} 
\label{sec:method-odometry}

Polygraph does not track cameras itself. Its input is the raw per-frame pose stream
$T^{\mathrm{raw}}_{s,i} \in \SE{3}$ produced by an off-the-shelf single-session
visual(-inertial) odometry backbone: ORB-SLAM3 \cite{campos2021orbslam3}, VINS-Fusion
\cite{qin2019vinsfusionlocal,qin2019vinsfusionglobal}, or a device's own onboard visual-inertial
odometry (a HoloLens or a Boston Dynamics Spot robot, for the indoor deployments in
\mychapterref{chap:experiments}). This stream is locally accurate but drifts over time, and is
expressed only in session $s$'s own local frame $\mathcal{F}_s$, with no relationship to any
other session's frame. \myfigref{fig:method-odometry} shows this raw stream against its own
selected keyframes for three real sessions.

A raw frame becomes a keyframe once it has moved far enough from the previous keyframe.
Writing $t_{s,i}, R_{s,i}$ for the translation and rotation components of
$T^{\mathrm{raw}}_{s,i}$, and $j$ for the index of the most recently selected keyframe, frame
$i$ becomes the next keyframe the first time
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:method-keyframe-trigger}
  \big\| t_{s,i} - t_{s,k_j} \big\| \;>\; \tau_{\mathrm{trans}}
  \quad\text{or}\quad
  \angle\!\big(R_{s,k_j}^{-1} R_{s,i}\big) \;>\; \tau_{\mathrm{rot}},
\end{equation}
where $\angle(R)$ denotes the rotation angle of $R \in \SO{3}$, with production values for
$\tau_{\mathrm{trans}}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{rot}}$ in \mytabref{tab:method-odometry-thresholds}.
Every subsequent stage of the pipeline operates purely on the resulting keyframe subsequence
$K_s$, never on raw per-frame poses. \myfigref{fig:method-odometry-zoom} zooms into this trigger
on ten consecutive raw frames of one real sequence.

\section{Online Clustering}
\label{sec:method-clustering}

Each session's own \texttt{ClusterBuilder} groups its keyframe stream into spatially bounded
\emph{clusters}, entirely online and using only that session's own travel distance. No
global trajectory or cross-session information is needed at this stage. A cluster
$C = (k_{j_1}, \dots, k_{j_2})$ starts at keyframe $k_{j_1}$ and closes at the first later
keyframe $k_{j_2}$ satisfying both
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:method-cluster-close}
  j_2 - j_1 + 1 \;\ge\; n_{\min}
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \Big( \big\| t_{s,j_2} - t_{s,j_1} \big\| \;>\; d_{\max} \;\;\text{or}\;\; j_2 - j_1 + 1 =
  n_{\max} \Big),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the cluster needs at least $n_{\min}$ keyframes \emph{and} either to have travelled more
than $d_{\max}$ from its own start pose or to have reached $n_{\max}$ keyframes outright.
Once $C$ closes, its own last $n_{\mathrm{ov}}$ keyframes seed the next cluster's initial
members, so consecutive clusters share keyframes and chain with no coverage gap between them.
\mytabref{tab:method-clustering-thresholds} lists the production values for all four.

\begin{table}[htb]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular*}{\textwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}} lllp{7cm}}
  \hline
  \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Role} \\
  \hline
  $n_{\min}$ & minimum cluster size & $6$ keyframes &
    a cluster cannot close before reaching this many keyframes, regardless of distance
    travelled (\eqref{eq:method-cluster-close}) \\
  $d_{\max}$ & closing distance & $10$m &
    once $n_{\min}$ is met, travelling this far from the cluster's own start pose closes it \\
  $n_{\max}$ & maximum cluster size & $15$ keyframes &
    closes the cluster outright once reached, even if $d_{\max}$ was never crossed \\
  $n_{\mathrm{ov}}$ & overlap keyframes & $2$ keyframes &
    how many of a closing cluster's own last keyframes seed the next cluster, so consecutive

Every keyframe, at the moment it is added to a cluster, is also assigned one global image
descriptor $d_{s,k} = f_{\mathrm{retrieval\_method}}(I_{s,k}) \in \mathbb{R}^D$
\cite{berton2025megaloc}, needed by cluster matching (\mysecref{sec:method-cluster-matching})
the moment the cluster closes. The output of this stage is a growing, per-session sequence of
closed clusters, each with its member keyframes and their descriptors.

\section{Cluster Matching (Retrieval)}
\label{sec:method-cluster-matching}

Checking a closed cluster against every other cluster in every other session with full
geometric verification would work, but it would be far too slow to run online. The per pair
verification in \mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation} involves real feature matching, RANSAC,
and a depth network, and none of that is cheap. Cluster matching exists to avoid doing that
expensive check on every possible pair. It is a fast, approximate filter, built entirely out
of image retrieval, whose only job is to narrow a huge field of possible cluster pairs down to
a short list of candidates worth actually verifying. Sometimes, it is allowed to be wrong (that
is exactly what \mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation} is for), but it has to be cheap enough to
run every time a cluster closes, for every session, for the entire lifetime of the pipeline.

Before going into the mechanics, the most important thing to understand about this stage is
that a newly closed cluster $C$ is matched against \emph{every} other eligible session, not
just one. A candidate group in session $s'$ is never compared or ranked against a group of
candidates in a different session $s''$. The notation below keeps this fixed throughout, $s$
denotes a session and $C$ one of its clusters, with $s$ usually the session the current cluster
just closed in and $s'$ the other session it is being matched against, and correspondingly for
their clusters.

\subsection{Per session matching}

For one other eligible session $s'$, in order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Each keyframe of $C$ queries $s'$'s own keyframes,} and each member keyframe
    $k \in C$ already has its own global descriptor, computed back in
    \mysecref{sec:method-clustering} the moment it was added to a cluster. That descriptor is
    compared, by cosine similarity, against the descriptors of every keyframe belonging to
    $s'$, and only $s'$. Keyframes belonging to any other session are not part of this
    comparison at all.
  \item \textbf{Each keyframe casts its votes, within $s'$ only.} Keyframe $k$ does not just
    look at its single best match in $s'$. Instead, it takes its $k_{\mathrm{top}}$ nearest neighbours
    \emph{within $s'$ specifically}, looks up which cluster each of those neighbours belongs
    to, and casts one vote for each of those parent clusters. A vote only counts towards
    anything if its underlying similarity clears the per-vote floor $\tau_{\mathrm{vote}}$
    (Table~\ref{tab:method-cluster-matching-thresholds}), so an implausibly weak match cannot
    buy a cluster a vote just by being the least-bad option available in $s'$. Because this
    whole procedure repeats independently for every other eligible session, one keyframe can
    cast up to $k_{\mathrm{top}}$ votes \emph{per session}, not $k_{\mathrm{top}}$ votes in
    total across the pipeline. With several other eligible sessions, the total number of votes
    one keyframe casts is $k_{\mathrm{top}}$ multiplied by however many other sessions are
    eligible.
  \item \textbf{The adjacency guard removes trivial self-matches, if $s'$ happens to be $C$'s
    own session.} $C$'s own last two same-session clusters are removed from candidacy outright,
    before anything else in this list happens. This step only ever does anything when $s'$ is
    the session $C$ itself belongs to. For every other session it has nothing to remove.
  \item \textbf{A session-level participation floor decides whether $s'$ is worth considering
    at all.} Across every vote just cast in $s'$, the pipeline counts how many \emph{distinct}
    keyframes of $C$ managed to cast at least one surviving vote there. If that count is below
    $n_{\mathrm{part}}$ (Table~\ref{tab:method-cluster-matching-thresholds}), $s'$ is dropped
    from candidacy entirely for this cluster. None of its candidate clusters are considered
    further, no matter how high any individual one's score would otherwise have been. This
    check looks only at how many of $C$'s keyframes engaged with $s'$ at all, not at which
    specific candidate cluster they voted for.
  \item \textbf{Surviving votes are pooled per candidate cluster and turned into a vote
    share.} Assuming $s'$ passed the step above, its votes are tallied up separately for every
    candidate cluster $C'$ that received at least one, and each is scored by
    \eqref{eq:method-vote-share}. Not how many raw votes it collected, but what fraction of
    \emph{all} the votes cast within $s'$ it collected.
  \item \textbf{Only the best few candidates, above a score floor, survive.} Candidates are
    ranked by vote share, and only those whose share also clears $\tau_{\mathrm{share}}$
    (Table~\ref{tab:method-cluster-matching-thresholds}) are kept at all. Of those, only the
    top $k_{\mathrm{clusters}}$ scoring candidates in $s'$ are carried forward into $C$'s
    final shortlist.
\end{enumerate}

The vote-share score from step 5 is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:method-vote-share}
  \mathrm{share}(C, C') \;=\; \frac{\text{votes cast for } C' \text{ within } s'}
  {\text{total votes cast within } s'},
\end{equation}
for a candidate cluster $C'$ that belongs to session $s'$. This is deliberately \emph{not} a
fraction of $C$'s own keyframe count. A session where $C$'s keyframes cast many votes overall,
spread thinly across many different candidate clusters, makes it harder for any single
candidate to earn a large share of that session's pool. A session where most of the votes
converge on one candidate makes that candidate's share close to $1$. This rewards agreement
relative to whatever else was on offer within that same session, rather than rewarding a
candidate just for existing in a session with few plausible alternatives to compete against.

    (step 2) \\

Two of these thresholds are easy to conflate, so it is worth spelling out the difference
directly. The participation floor $n_{\mathrm{part}}$ (step 4) and the vote-share floor
$\tau_{\mathrm{share}}$ (step 6) answer two different questions, checked at two different
points, about two different things. The participation floor asks, before any individual
candidate is even scored, did enough of $C$'s own keyframes find anything worth voting for in
$s'$ at all? It exists to protect against a mostly irrelevant session sneaking a candidate
through on the strength of one noisy, coincidentally-similar keyframe pair, when the rest of
$C$ found nothing there. Its production value is deliberately permissive. $s'$ only needs a
single one of $C$'s keyframes to have voted for something to stay in play at all, not a
majority of them. The vote-share floor, by contrast, is only ever checked once a session has
already passed that first test, and it asks a narrower question about one specific candidate,
given that $s'$ is worth considering at all, is this particular candidate's own share of the
vote pool high enough on its own?

The adjacency guard (step 3) exists because of how clustering itself works
(\mysecref{sec:method-clustering}). Consecutive clusters in the same session deliberately
share their last few keyframes, so that clusters chain together with no coverage gap between
them. Without the guard, whenever $s'$ happens to be $C$'s own session, $C$'s immediately
preceding or following cluster would trivially win every single time. They overlap in actual
keyframes with $C$, so they would collect an outsized share of that session's vote pool by
construction, not because the session was ever genuinely revisited. That is not a real cluster
match, it is just the clustering scheme's own stitching showing through, so those two
neighbouring clusters are removed from candidacy before scoring even begins. And this is only for
$C$'s own session, every other eligible session is unaffected by this guard entirely.

Once this whole per-session procedure has run once for every other eligible session, and
their surviving candidates have been pooled together, the result is a short list of
candidate cluster pairs $(C, C')$, one list per newly closed cluster,
pooled across however many other sessions had at least one surviving candidate. It is
important to be clear about what that list is, and what it is not. It is a list of pairs
worth checking, not a list of confirmed matches. Retrieval descriptors are trained to
recognize that two images look similar, not to prove that they were taken at the same physical
place. Generic-looking scenes, repeated architectural elements, or two genuinely different
locations that simply resemble each other can all produce a high vote share without any real
revisit behind them. Every candidate this stage proposes still has to earn its way into the
pose graph through actual geometric verification, which is exactly what
\mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation} does next. \myfigref{fig:method-cluster-matching} shows one
real closed cluster and its four surviving candidates from a production HYDRO run.

\section{Joint Pose Estimation}
\label{sec:method-pose-estimation}

Cluster matching only proposes candidate pairs. This stage verifies them geometrically and is
the only stage that actually looks at image content and produces metric poses.

\subsection{Keyframe-Pair Selection}

Checking every keyframe in $C$ against every keyframe in $C'$ would be wasteful and redundant. Instead, every cross keyframe-pair $(k,k') \in C \times C'$ is weighted by descriptor similarity, and a maximum-spanning tree (MST) over this complete bipartite similarity graph selects the $k_{\mathrm{pairs}}$ highest-similarity, spatially diverse pairs to actually verify. The MST property guarantees that every selected pair covers at least one keyframe no earlier selected pair did, so the selected pairs spread across the cluster instead of collapsing onto one dominant viewpoint. The production value is $k_{\mathrm{pairs}} = 10$.

\subsection{Per-Pair Verification}

Each selected pair $(k, k')$ goes through the same six-step verification:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sparse matching}: extraction of XFeat features \cite{potje2024xfeat} followed by
    LighterGlue matching, a lighter, faster retraining of LightGlue
    \cite{lindenberger2023lightglue} distributed as part of the XFeat project, giving a
 set of correspondences $M$.
  \item \textbf{Essential-matrix RANSAC} (via poselib) on $M$, giving a scale-free relative
    rotation $\hat{R}$ and translation direction $\hat{t}$ (unit norm) plus an inlier subset
    $M_{\mathrm{in}} \subseteq M$. The pair is already rejected outright here, before
    triangulation or depth are computed for it at all, if fewer than $n_{\mathrm{sf}}$ matches
    survive as inliers or this RANSAC's own model score exceeds $\epsilon_{\mathrm{sf}}$.
  \item \textbf{Triangulation} (DLT) of $M_{\mathrm{in}}$ under $(\hat{R}, \hat{t})$, giving
    scale-free 3D points $\{X_i^{\mathrm{tri}}\}$ in $k$'s own camera frame, correct only up to
    one unknown global scale.
  \item \textbf{Monocular metric depth lift}: Metric3D v2 \cite{hu2024metric3dv2} predicts a
    metric depth map for $k$ and $k'$ independently. Back-projecting each matched keypoint
    through its own image's depth map and camera intrinsics gives two separate metric 3D point
    sets, not one, $\{X_i^{\mathrm{depth}}\}$ from $k$'s own depth map, in $k$'s camera frame
    (the same frame the triangulated points already live in), and $\{X_i^{\prime\mathrm{depth}}\}$
    from $k'$'s own depth map, in $k'$'s camera frame. A point whose predicted depth falls
    outside a plausible metric range is already discarded at this step.
  \item \textbf{Scale-consistency filter}: this check looks at each correspondence entirely on
    its own, not against any other correspondence, and only ever compares quantities already
    expressed in $k$'s own camera frame, the triangulated point $X_i^{\mathrm{tri}}$ against
    $k$'s own depth-lifted point $X_i^{\mathrm{depth}}$, never against $k'$'s. For
    correspondence $i$, a per-point scale factor $\hat{s}_i$ is estimated as the mean, over the
    three coordinate axes, of the depth-lifted point's coordinates divided by the triangulated
    point's coordinates,
    $\hat{s}_i = \mathrm{mean}\big(X_i^{\mathrm{depth}} \oslash X_i^{\mathrm{tri}}\big)$ (with
    $\oslash$ elementwise division). Scaling the triangulated point by $\hat{s}_i$ and
    comparing it directly against the depth-lifted point gives a residual
    $r_i = \| X_i^{\mathrm{depth}} - \hat{s}_i X_i^{\mathrm{tri}} \|$, in metres. The
    correspondence $i$ is kept only if $r_i$ clears the scale-consistency floor $\tau_{\mathrm{scale}}$
    (production value $0.2$m). Intuitively, a genuinely correct correspondence's triangulated
    and depth-lifted positions should point in the same direction and differ by one consistent
    scale across all three axes. If they do, a single per-point scale factor explains the
    difference well and the residual is small. A bad triangulation or a mismatched
    correspondence instead tends to disagree with itself internally, its own per-axis ratios
    do not agree on one consistent scale, so forcing a single mean scale factor onto it and
    comparing back leaves a large residual. What the filter actually catches is internal
    inconsistency within one correspondence, not disagreement between different
    correspondences.
  \item \textbf{PnP} (via poselib): the correspondences that survive step 5 hand their $k'$-side
    depth-lifted metric points $X_i^{\prime\mathrm{depth}}$, not the $k$-side points the
    scale-consistency filter just validated, to a PnP solve against their 2D pixel location in
    $k$. This directly yields $\hat{T}_{k,k'} \in \SE{3}$, $k$ expressed relative to $k'$, with
    no extra inversion needed, since the 3D points already live in $k'$'s frame and the 2D
    observations are $k$'s own pixels.
\end{enumerate}
Two separate accept/reject gates apply along this chain, not just one at the end. Step 2's
essential-matrix RANSAC already discards a pair outright, well before triangulation or depth are
computed for it, if fewer than $n_{\mathrm{sf}} = 10$ matches survive as its own inliers or its
own model score exceeds $\epsilon_{\mathrm{sf}} = 0.5$. A pair that survives this far is still
rejected outright unless it also clears three further conditions. At least $n_{\mathrm{valid}} =
150$ correspondences must survive triangulation, depth-validity, and the scale-consistency filter
combined. The final, absolute-pose (PnP) RANSAC's own inlier ratio must reach at least
$\rho_{\mathrm{abs}} = 0.80$. That same RANSAC's model score must stay at most
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{abs}} = 0.05$.

\subsection{Translation-Consensus Voting}

The surviving per-pair estimates $\{\hat{T}_1, \dots, \hat{T}_m\}$ (of the up to
$k_{\mathrm{pairs}}$ attempted) are combined by a translation-consensus vote rather than
trusted individually. Two estimates \emph{agree} if their implied translations differ by less
than $\tau_{\mathrm{agree}} = 1.5$m. Every estimate is scored by how many other estimates it
agrees with. The estimate with the most agreeing neighbors becomes the anchor of the consensus
set, which also includes every other estimate that agrees with \emph{that anchor}. This is a
mode-seeking vote in the style of RANSAC's own inlier counting, not a requirement that every
pair of estimates within the consensus set mutually agree with each other. Two non-anchor
members can end up in the same consensus set without agreeing with one another, as long as both
agree with the anchor. The consensus set is then averaged into one confirmed edge measurement
$\hat{z}$ via a chordal $\SE{3}$ mean, translations averaged arithmetically and rotations averaged
by projecting their arithmetic mean onto $\SO{3}$ (via SVD). A candidate pair of clusters only
becomes a confirmed edge once the consensus set holds at least $n_{\mathrm{vote}} = 2$
estimates. A single, uncorroborated estimate is never enough, and the candidate is otherwise
dropped. When multi-edge consensus is enabled (the production setting used throughout this
thesis), every individual estimate in the consensus set is \emph{also} added as its own extra
graph edge alongside the averaged one, giving the optimizer more redundant evidence per
confirmed match.

\section{The Two-Tier Consensus Gate}
\label{sec:method-gate}

A confirmed edge from \mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation} is not unconditionally admitted to the shared
pose graph. This section describes the gate that decides whether it is or when.

\subsection{Same-Session Edges Bypass the Gate}

An edge whose two clusters both belong to the same session commits directly to the graph,
no check required. That session's own position is already fully determined by its own
sequential odometry chain (\mysecref{sec:method-overview}'s $E_{\mathrm{odom}}$), so a
same session loop closure ($E_{\mathrm{intra}}$) is simply extra corroborating evidence, not
new placement information.

\subsection{Anchor/Query Assignment}

Before a confirmed cross-session edge between session $s$ and session $s'$ can be checked at all, the gate first has to decide which of the two sessions to trust. One side plays anchor, meaning its current pose estimate is treated as fixed and correct for this check, and the other plays query, meaning it is the side actually being tested against that fixed reference.

That choice is made purely by how well-established each session is already. Let $n(s)$ denote the number of currently live (unpruned) cross-session edges already incident on session $s$, a simple proxy for how much prior cross-session evidence already supports that session's position. Whichever of $s$, $s'$ has the larger $n(\cdot)$ becomes anchor, the other becomes query. If $n(s) = n(s')$, most commonly because neither session has any live cross-session edges yet (two sessions meeting for the first time), the tie is broken with no real criterion at all, whichever session happened to be listed first internally keeps the anchor role. This is arbitrary in that sense, but still deterministic, the same tie always resolves the same way, so re-running the pipeline on the same data reproduces the same assignment. This particular tie case is harmless. When neither session has any live edges, the candidate is routed to Tier 1 below, which never actually leans on either side's pose estimate, it only checks whether several independent candidates agree with each other, so it does not matter which of the two was labeled anchor.

The query session's own edge count then decides how the candidate is checked. Whether it already has at least one live cross-session edge selects which of the two tiers below it is routed through.


\subsection{Tier 1: Corroboration Pool}

If the query session is still fully disconnected ($n(\text{query}) = 0$), there is yet no live
estimate worth checking against a single candidate. Instead, candidates targeting that
session are pooled, and checked only against each other. Once a subset of at least
$m_{\mathrm{corrob}} = 2$ pooled candidates mutually agree (their implied world poses within
$\tau_{\mathrm{pool}} = 2.0$m / $10.0°$ of one another) \emph{and} are independent (their
source clusters on the anchor side at least $g_{\min} = 3$ clusters apart, so two candidates
are not simply the same retrieval mistake repeated), they commit together as a group and
jointly establish that session's first anchor, arriving at $\ge 2$ live cross-session edges at
once, never at exactly one. A candidate that never finds enough agreeing peers is dropped once
it has sat in the pool longer than $t_{\max} = 1$ day. This parameter was never actually hit in our testing, but we left it in for completeness.

\subsection{Tier 2: Pending Queue}

If the query session already has at least one live cross-session edge, the candidate is
checked immediately against the query's current live pose estimate, rather than queued first.
The consistency threshold depends on how much existing evidence that session already has:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:method-consistency-table}
  \big(\tau_{\mathrm{trans}}(n), \tau_{\mathrm{rot}}(n)\big) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    (\infty, \infty) & n = 0 \quad\text{(bootstrap row, unreachable in Tier 2 by
      construction)} \\
    (10.0\text{m}, 20.0°) & n \ge 1,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $n = n(\text{query})$. If the pose the candidate implies is consistent with the query's
current estimate under \eqref{eq:method-consistency-table}, it commits immediately and never
touches the queue. If it fails, it is placed in the pending queue and re-checked against the
graph's estimate at every later full update (\mysecref{sec:method-graph}) until it either
passes, accumulates $r_{\max} = 3$ rejections, or ages out past $t_{\max}$ (whichever comes
first), at which point it is dropped for good.

\subsection{Worked Example}

Consider three cross-session candidates between sessions A and B, arriving in order. Both
sessions start with $n(\cdot) = 0$, so edge 1 (A$\leftrightarrow$B) ties for anchor. A wins
arbitrarily, and B is query with $n(\mathrm{B})=0$, routing to Tier 1. Its corroboration pool for B now
has one entry, below $m_{\mathrm{corrob}}=2$, so nothing is committed yet. Edge 2 (a different A
cluster $\leftrightarrow$ B cluster) runs in the same way, and the pool for B now holds two
entries. If they agree and are independent, both commit together right now, and A and B
jump straight to $2$ live cross-session edges. The count is symmetric, and the committed edge is
incident on both of its sessions, not only the query side. If they disagree, both stay pooled,
waiting for a future third candidate to pair with one of them. If none ever arrives, both
simply age out, and B is never anchored via this pair.

Assuming that edges 1 and 2 corroborated, a third candidate A$\leftrightarrow$ B (edge 3) now
finds $n(\mathrm{B}) \ge 1$ (Tier 2) and is checked immediately against the live estimate of B under
the $10.0$m / $20.0°$ limit of \eqref{eq:method-consistency-table}. If consistent, it commits
immediately, and B (and A) grow one edge at a time from here on, never in pairs again. If not,
it lands in the pending queue for a later re-check.

\subsection{Fast and Full Updates}

Two solves keep the graph current, at different costs and cadences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fast update}: an incremental Bayes-tree solve (iSAM2 \cite{kaess2012isam2}),
    folding in every new keyframe vertex and every newly admitted edge after each closed
    cluster.
  \item \textbf{Full update}: a batch nonlinear least-squares solve over the whole graph,
    run every $n_{\mathrm{slow}} = 10$ closed clusters. For every loop-closure edge $e$
    (intra- or cross-session), let $r_e$ be its residual under
    \eqref{eq:method-pgo-objective}; $e$ is pruned if
    \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:method-outlier-prune}
      r_e \;>\; \kappa \cdot \mathrm{median}\big(\{ r_{e'} : e' \text{ same group as } e \}\big),
    \end{equation}
    with $\kappa = 3$ and \emph{group} meaning intra-session edges are judged against their own
    median, cross-session edges against theirs, so one session's tighter loop closures
    cannot drag down the bar for everyone else's. This step also drains the pending queue
    (\mysecref{sec:method-gate}) against the freshly re-solved estimate.
    \myfigref{fig:method-graph-outliers} shows the real residual distribution and the resulting
    32 kept / 21 pruned split from one full update on a production KITTI session.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{End-of-Run Finalization}

Once every frame across every session has been processed, one more full update runs
unconditionally, not gated by the cluster count trigger like the periodic ones, and
drains the pending queue once more. Only then do two final passes run, once each, over the
complete graph:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inter-cluster pair consensus}: every session pair's independent edges are
    cross-checked against each other using each edge's pre-fusion local pose (the
    raw-odometry-derived pose captured when the edge was created, never touched by any
    optimization pass) rather than the graph's current estimate. This is deliberate,
    \eqref{eq:method-outlier-prune} is structurally blind to a group of edges that are
    individually plausible and mutually self-consistent, since least-squares optimization
    simply bends the graph to satisfy the majority, producing small residuals against the
    graph's own (possibly wrong) current estimate no matter how wrong that estimate collectively
    is. Comparing against each edge's fixed, un-warped local pose instead gives a reference the
    group could not have already corrupted.
  \item \textbf{Final GNC-TLS solve}: one archival Graduated Non-Convexity truncated
    least-squares pass \cite{yang2020gnc} over the complete graph, including edges
    already pruned by the full update, giving every loop-closure edge one last principled
    outlier weight and producing the pipeline's final pose estimate. It inherits the same
    blind spot as \eqref{eq:method-outlier-prune} for the same reason (it is still only
    weighting by residual against the graph's own current solve), which is exactly why the
    inter-cluster pair consensus step above has to run first.
\end{enumerate}
The end-of-run order is therefore fixed: full update (removes obvious single-edge outliers)
$\to$ drain the pending queue (last chance for anything still waiting) $\to$ inter-cluster pair
consensus (catches hidden, mutually self-consistent outlier groups) $\to$ final GNC-TLS solve
(final weights for everything that survives). \myfigref{fig:method-graph-solve} shows what this
buys concretely, one real session's raw odometry against its own trajectory after the graph is
solved.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:method-summary}

Return to \mysecref{sec:method-overview}'s problem statement. The output of this pipeline
is one pose graph spanning every session that has been shown, with corroborated evidence, to
revisit another, with each session's own trajectory corrected wherever it overlaps with the
rest. It is built up incrementally, one keyframe and one closed cluster at a time, with no
requirement that every session be collected before processing starts, and no session ever
designated a fixed reference in advance. \mychapterref{chap:experiments} evaluates this
pipeline across the datasets introduced in \mysecref{sec:relatedwork-2go}.
