\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:relatedwork}

\section{Visual(-Inertial) Odometry and Single-Session SLAM}

Polygraph is built on top of, rather than instead of, single-session visual and
visual-inertial SLAM. Systems such as ORB-SLAM3 \cite{campos2021orbslam3} and VINS-Fusion
\cite{qin2019vinsfusionlocal,qin2019vinsfusionglobal} track a single session's camera (optionally fused with IMU
measurements) in real time, producing a locally accurate but drift-affected trajectory
together with intra-session loop closures. This thesis treats such a system's raw per-frame
pose stream as Polygraph's input odometry (\mysecref{sec:method-odometry}) and does not
modify it; Polygraph's contribution begins where single-session tracking ends, at the
problem of relating \emph{different} sessions' independently-tracked trajectories to one
another. The incremental smoothing formulation these systems' backends use for pose-graph
optimization --- most directly iSAM2 \cite{kaess2012isam2}, built on the factor-graph
formulation underlying GTSAM --- is also what Polygraph's own online graph solve
(\mysecref{sec:method-graph}) is built on.

\section{Visual Place Recognition for Loop Closure}

Deciding \emph{which} pair of keyframes might correspond to the same physical place, before
any geometric verification is attempted, is a visual place recognition (retrieval) problem:
each image is embedded into a global descriptor, and nearest-neighbor search over previously
seen descriptors proposes revisit candidates. NetVLAD \cite{arandjelovic2016netvlad}
established the learned aggregation approach this line of work builds on; more recent
descriptors such as SALAD \cite{izquierdo2024salad}, BoQ \cite{alibey2024boq}, and MegaLoc
\cite{berton2025megaloc} improve retrieval accuracy and robustness to viewpoint and
appearance change. Polygraph uses one such descriptor (\mysecref{sec:method-clustering}) to
cast votes between clusters of keyframes across sessions; unlike single-session loop closure,
where the retrieval index only ever needs to answer queries against one session's own past,
Polygraph's retrieval index spans every other session concurrently and must scale as new
sessions --- not just new keyframes within a session --- are added online.

\section{Learned Two-View Matching and Metric Pose Recovery}

A retrieval hit is only a hypothesis; Polygraph verifies each candidate keyframe pair
geometrically before it is allowed to become a graph edge
(\mysecref{sec:method-pose-estimation}). Sparse feature matching between the two images uses
XFeat \cite{potje2024xfeat} for feature extraction and LighterGlue for matching --- a lighter,
faster retraining of LightGlue \cite{lindenberger2023lightglue} distributed as part of the
XFeat project \cite{potje2024xfeat}; the overall recipe of sparse matching lifted to metric
scale with a monocular depth prior matches 2GO's own default (``CL-Absolute'') variant
\cite{lim2025twogo}. Because
two-view matching alone only recovers relative pose up to an unknown scale, Polygraph lifts matched
keypoints to metric 3D with a monocular metric depth estimator, Metric3D
\cite{yin2023metric3d}, and checks that the resulting metric estimate agrees with the
scale-free triangulated one before accepting a match --- avoiding the need for a second,
overlapping camera or a dense stereo/LiDAR sensor purely to resolve scale. At the pose-graph
level, once candidate edges are admitted, Polygraph's final robust solve uses Graduated
Non-Convexity with a truncated least-squares cost (GNC-TLS) \cite{yang2020gnc} to down-weight
or reject edges that remain outliers even after the gating described in
\mysecref{sec:method-gate}.

\section{Multi-Session and Lifelong Mapping}

Fusing more than one session's map is a long-standing problem beyond single-session SLAM.
Systems that maintain dense or semi-dense multi-map representations, such as ORB-SLAM3's
Atlas \cite{campos2021orbslam3}, merge sessions by matching and aligning their dense maps
directly, which gives strong geometric evidence for a match but ties the cost of fusion to
the cost of maintaining dense geometry across every session. Polygraph instead follows the
sparse, pose-graph-only line of work described next, trading some of that direct geometric
evidence for a representation whose cost scales with the number of keyframes and sessions,
not with scene density.

\section{2GO: Sparse Multi-Session Pose-Graph Fusion}
\label{sec:relatedwork-2go}

The closest prior work to this thesis is 2GO \cite{lim2025twogo}, which fuses multiple
sessions' odometry into one pose graph using exactly the ingredients described above:
retrieval-based candidate proposal, sparse matching with a monocular depth prior for metric
scale, and a two-tier gate that only admits a cross-session edge once it is corroborated ---
first against other pending candidates for a session with no prior anchor, then against the
graph's live estimate once a session has one. Several components of Polygraph's own gating
and consistency-checking logic (\mysecref{sec:method-gate}) directly mirror 2GO's, including
its per-session-count consistency thresholds and its optional multi-edge consensus scheme,
ported from 2GO's own published parameterization rather than independently re-derived here.

Where Polygraph departs from 2GO is architectural. 2GO's formulation assumes every session is
available before fusion begins and designates one session in advance as the graph's fixed
reference frame; Polygraph removes both assumptions, giving every session its own
independent graph component with its own gauge-fixing prior at creation time, so that
sessions can be processed one at a time, in any order, as they are collected
(\mysecref{sec:method-graph}). 2GO's own published evaluation is on KITTI, the VBR benchmark
\cite{brizi2024vbr}, and a self-recorded indoor session captured with a Boston Dynamics Spot
robot; it does not cover the CroCoDL- and LaMAR-derived indoor deployments or the RTK-verified
Oxford RobotCar sessions this thesis evaluates on (\mychapterref{chap:experiments}), none of
which --- to the best of our knowledge --- any prior multi-session pose-graph fusion system
has been evaluated against.
\mysecref{sec:experiments-2go-comparison} reports a direct, hardware-matched comparison
against 2GO's own published KITTI numbers, including an open gap in loop-closure recall that
this thesis surfaces rather than resolves.

\section{Datasets for Multi-Session Evaluation}

KITTI \cite{geiger2012kitti} is a standard, widely used outdoor driving benchmark, used here
in its multi-sequence form to construct multi-session fusion scenarios. Oxford RobotCar
\cite{maddern2017robotcar}, with the later RTK-based ground truth release
\cite{maddern2020rtk}, provides repeated traversals of the same route recorded months apart
under different weather, season, and lighting; this thesis draws a paired easy/hard session
split from it --- sharing all but one session between the two, with the hard split adding a
night traversal --- specifically to test single- and multi-session performance under real
day/night appearance change, an axis prior work on this dataset has approached from other
angles, including radar-based SLAM \cite{radarslam2021}, direct sparse odometry with
omnidirectional cameras \cite{matsuki2018omnidso}, and a systematic evaluation of visual
odometry robustness in rain \cite{tan2023voautorain}, but never for multi-session pose-graph
fusion. The two indoor deployments evaluated in this thesis are built from existing
localization/mapping benchmarks rather than newly captured: HGE from CroCoDL
\cite{blum2025crocodl}, a cross-device dataset combining HoloLens and legged-robot recordings,
and HYDRO from LaMAR \cite{sarlin2022lamar}, a benchmark for AR localization and mapping
across long, unconstrained indoor/outdoor trajectories --- neither previously used to evaluate
multi-session pose-graph fusion. All three are introduced together with the rest of the
experimental setup in \mychapterref{chap:experiments}; EuRoC \cite{burri2016euroc} is also
part of the broader experimental campaign (\mysecref{sec:experiments-euroc}) but is not
treated as a headline dataset in this chapter, since its evaluation is still evolving.
